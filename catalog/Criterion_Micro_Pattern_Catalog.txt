Criterion Micro-Pattern Catalog
Source: Evaluator Patterns: Contractual Relationships Built from Prompts, Claims, and Knowledge Graphs
Author: Granville "Randy" Miller
Catalog Version: 1.0
Generated from Section 4.0 of the source paper

PURPOSE

This catalog contains twelve reusable criterion micro-patterns for constructing evaluators. A response is divided into claims, each claim is classified by knowledge dimension, and the corresponding criterion micro-pattern is selected to evaluate that claim.

Knowledge dimensions classify the kind of knowledge asserted by a claim:
- Factual knowledge
- Conceptual knowledge
- Procedural knowledge
- Metacognitive knowledge

Three additional patterns are cross-cutting:
- Deontic Status Consistency
- Artifact Structure
- Citation

A claim and a criterion are different objects:
- A claim is content extracted from a response.
- A criterion is a contractual clause in an evaluator that binds a claim to ground truth and makes it testable.

The same instantiated criterion may be placed in one of three evaluator sections:
- Required claims: The claim must be present and correct.
- Supporting claims: The claim is optional, but if present, it must be correct.
- Disallowed claims: The claim must not appear.

======================================================================
KNOWLEDGE-DIMENSION CLASSIFICATION
======================================================================

1. FACTUAL KNOWLEDGE

Definition:
The basic, discrete elements that a system must know to be acquainted with a discipline or solve problems. Factual knowledge consists of isolated facts that are meaningful on their own without requiring understanding of a larger structure.

Associated criterion micro-patterns:
- Factual Assertion
- Contextual

2. CONCEPTUAL KNOWLEDGE

Definition:
Knowledge of interrelationships among basic elements within a larger structure that enable those elements to function together. Conceptual knowledge concerns frameworks, categories, principles, classifications, and relationships.

Associated criterion micro-patterns:
- Conceptual Relation
- Definitional

3. PROCEDURAL KNOWLEDGE

Definition:
Knowledge of how to do something, including methods of inquiry and the criteria for using skills, algorithms, techniques, and methods. Procedural knowledge commonly takes the form of steps, ordering constraints, transformations, and applicability conditions.

Associated criterion micro-patterns:
- Procedural Step Presence
- Procedural Ordering
- Procedural Transformation
- Procedural Guard

4. METACOGNITIVE KNOWLEDGE

Definition:
Knowledge about cognition, knowledge, confidence, scope, and the limits of what is known. It is reflexive knowledge about knowing rather than knowledge of domain facts, structures, or methods.

Associated criterion micro-pattern:
- Epistemic Status Consistency

5. CROSS-CUTTING CONCERNS

Definition:
These patterns do not correspond to one knowledge dimension. They augment factual, conceptual, procedural, or metacognitive criteria.

Associated criterion micro-patterns:
- Deontic Status Consistency
- Artifact Structure
- Citation

======================================================================
CRITERION MICRO-PATTERNS
======================================================================

4.1 FACTUAL ASSERTION
Knowledge dimension: Factual Knowledge

Intent:
Verify a discrete, verifiable fact such as an entity, value, date, title, identifier, attribution, medium, location, or citation.

Claim-identification guidance:
Use this type when a statement can be checked as a standalone fact and does not primarily assert a relationship, definition, process, judgment of certainty, obligation, structural requirement, or citation behavior.

Claim-type label:
Factual Assertion

Criterion template:
The response states [fact F].

Generic examples:
- The response states the name of the artist.
- The response identifies the date of the work.
- The response states that the work is a woodcut.
- The response identifies the museum that holds the work.

Classification cues:
- Names
- Dates
- Titles
- Quantities
- Locations
- Identifiers
- Attributions
- Discrete properties
- Verifiable events

Do not use when:
- The statement defines a term; use Definitional.
- The statement asserts a relationship or rationale; use Conceptual Relation.
- The statement provides optional background that situates another claim; consider Contextual.
- The statement describes a mandatory step; use Procedural Step Presence.

4.2 CONTEXTUAL
Knowledge dimension: Factual Knowledge

Intent:
Verify background conditions that situate other claims. The content is factual in kind but peripheral to the central answer or material conclusion.

Claim-identification guidance:
Use this type for supporting historical, biographical, cultural, institutional, or situational facts that help explain the main claim but are not themselves the principal answer.

Claim-type label:
Contextual

Criterion template:
The response situates [claim C] in [background B].

Generic examples:
- The response notes the historical circumstances surrounding an artwork.
- The response places an artistic technique within a broader printmaking tradition.
- The response supplies biographical background relevant to the interpretation.

Classification cues:
- Background
- Historical setting
- Biographical context
- Cultural context
- Peripheral explanatory facts
- Conditions surrounding the main issue

Do not use when:
- The fact is itself a required direct answer; use Factual Assertion.
- The statement expresses a causal, classificatory, or structural relationship; use Conceptual Relation.
- The statement concerns scope or certainty; use Epistemic Status Consistency.

4.3 CONCEPTUAL RELATION
Knowledge dimension: Conceptual Knowledge

Intent:
Verify a relationship, classification, principle, rationale, dependency, cause, distinction, or organizing structure. Rephrasing is allowed, but the underlying relation must not be changed.

Claim-identification guidance:
Use this type when the claim connects two or more concepts, explains how elements relate, classifies something within a framework, distinguishes categories, or states why something functions as it does.

Claim-type label:
Conceptual Relation

Criterion template:
The response expresses [relation R between X and Y], preserving the structure of the relation.

Generic examples:
- The response explains that repeated lines create visual rhythm.
- The response distinguishes factual knowledge from conceptual knowledge.
- The response relates carving technique to the resulting texture.
- The response classifies a claim as conceptual because it expresses a relationship among elements.

Classification cues:
- Causes
- Contributes to
- Results in
- Depends on
- Is a type of
- Differs from
- Is related to
- Is composed of
- Functions as
- Rationale or principle

Do not use when:
- The statement only asserts a discrete fact; use Factual Assertion.
- The statement gives necessary and sufficient conditions for a term; use Definitional.
- The statement describes a sequence of actions; use a procedural pattern.

4.4 DEFINITIONAL
Knowledge dimension: Conceptual Knowledge

Intent:
Verify a definition by checking the necessary and sufficient conditions, essential characteristics, or delimiting conditions attached to a term.

Claim-identification guidance:
Use this type when the response explains what a term means, what qualifies as an instance of a category, or what conditions distinguish the term from related concepts.

Claim-type label:
Definitional

Criterion template:
The response defines [term T] by [conditions].

Generic examples:
- The response defines a woodcut as a relief-printing process in which the raised surface receives ink.
- The response defines factual knowledge as discrete information meaningful on its own.
- The response defines an evaluator criterion as a contractual clause used to test a claim against ground truth.

Classification cues:
- Means
- Is defined as
- Refers to
- Consists of
- Requires
- Is characterized by
- Necessary and sufficient conditions
- Boundary between categories

Do not use when:
- The statement merely lists one attribute or fact about the term; use Factual Assertion.
- The statement relates concepts without defining one; use Conceptual Relation.

4.5 PROCEDURAL STEP PRESENCE
Knowledge dimension: Procedural Knowledge

Intent:
Verify that a mandatory step of a reference procedure appears in the response.

Claim-identification guidance:
Use this type when the response instructs the reader to perform a specific action that is required by the procedure, method, workflow, or algorithm.

Claim-type label:
Procedural Step Presence

Criterion template:
The response includes [step S] of the reference procedure.

Generic examples:
- The response instructs the user to prepare the block before carving.
- The response includes a step to validate the input file before processing it.
- The response includes a step to compare each response file with its matching prompt ID.

Classification cues:
- First, perform
- Check
- Record
- Submit
- Review
- Validate
- Save
- Include
- Execute a required action

Do not use when:
- The issue is whether steps occur in the correct order; use Procedural Ordering.
- The issue is whether an operation produces the correct result; use Procedural Transformation.
- The step applies only under stated conditions; pair with Procedural Guard.
- The statement presents an action as mandatory, advisory, permitted, or prohibited; also consider Deontic Status Consistency.

4.6 PROCEDURAL ORDERING
Knowledge dimension: Procedural Knowledge

Intent:
Verify that procedural steps respect required precedence, dependency, or sequencing constraints.

Claim-identification guidance:
Use this type when correctness depends not only on the presence of steps but also on one step occurring before, after, or conditionally following another.

Claim-type label:
Procedural Ordering

Criterion template:
The response orders [step Si] before [step Sj] where the reference requires it.

Generic examples:
- The response validates the CSV before submitting prompts to the API.
- The response reads the matching response file before constructing the combined analysis prompt.
- The response carves the block before applying ink and printing.

Classification cues:
- Before
- After
- Then
- Next
- Subsequently
- Precondition
- Dependency
- Required sequence

Do not use when:
- Only the presence of a step matters; use Procedural Step Presence.
- The claim concerns whether a transformation is correctly performed; use Procedural Transformation.
- The sequence is optional rather than required; classify according to the actual procedural requirement.

4.7 PROCEDURAL TRANSFORMATION
Knowledge dimension: Procedural Knowledge

Intent:
Verify that a step's operation is valid, including the correct operation, correct inputs, and correct intermediate or final result.

Claim-identification guidance:
Use this type when the response performs or describes a computation, conversion, derivation, mapping, filtering operation, classification operation, or transformation of inputs into outputs.

Claim-type label:
Procedural Transformation

Criterion template:
The response derives [output O] from [inputs I] via [operation Op].

Generic examples:
- The response derives a safe filename from the Prompt ID by replacing prohibited characters.
- The response maps each claim to a criterion micro-pattern using its knowledge dimension.
- The response transforms CSV rows into API requests and saves the returned text to matching files.
- The response calculates a value using the correct formula and inputs.

Classification cues:
- Calculates
- Converts
- Maps
- Derives
- Produces
- Filters
- Transforms
- Classifies according to a rule
- Uses inputs to generate an output

Do not use when:
- The claim only identifies a required action; use Procedural Step Presence.
- The issue is step sequence; use Procedural Ordering.
- The operation is valid only under a condition; pair with Procedural Guard.

4.8 PROCEDURAL GUARD
Knowledge dimension: Procedural Knowledge

Intent:
Verify that the applicability conditions, exceptions, preconditions, stopping conditions, or boundary conditions of a procedure are stated and respected.

Claim-identification guidance:
Use this type when a procedure, step, or rule applies only if a condition is satisfied or does not apply when an exception is present.

Claim-type label:
Procedural Guard

Criterion template:
The response conditions [step S] on [guard G].

Generic examples:
- The response skips an output file only if it already exists and overwrite is not enabled.
- The response retries an API request only for temporary or server-side failures.
- The response processes a row only when both Prompt ID and Prompt are present.
- The response warns rather than proceeds when the matching response file is missing.

Classification cues:
- If
- Unless
- Only when
- Except when
- Provided that
- In the event that
- Precondition
- Exception
- Boundary condition
- Stopping condition

Do not use when:
- The statement merely lists a step without conditions; use Procedural Step Presence.
- The condition expresses uncertainty about knowledge rather than applicability of a procedure; use Epistemic Status Consistency.

4.9 EPISTEMIC STATUS CONSISTENCY
Knowledge dimension: Metacognitive Knowledge

Intent:
Verify that the asserted certainty, confidence, source support, and scope of a claim match the reference's epistemic annotations, including acknowledging what the available corpus does not establish.

Claim-identification guidance:
Use this type for claims about certainty, uncertainty, limitations, assumptions, evidence sufficiency, source boundaries, inference, possibility, or the difference between documented fact and interpretation.

Claim-type label:
Epistemic Status Consistency

Criterion template:
The response represents [claim C] with epistemic status [status E] consistent with the reference.

Disallowed inversions:
- Overclaiming beyond the source or corpus.
- Presenting inference as established fact.
- Confident assertion at a knowledge-graph or corpus boundary.
- Underclaiming by hedging a fact that the reference establishes definitively.
- Expanding a scoped claim into a universal claim.

Generic examples:
- The response identifies an interpretation as interpretive rather than documented fact.
- The response states that the source does not establish a precise causal relationship.
- The response qualifies a conclusion when evidence is incomplete.
- The response states a documented date definitively rather than unnecessarily hedging it.

Classification cues:
- May
- Might
- Likely
- Appears
- Suggests
- The source establishes
- The source does not establish
- It is uncertain
- This is an interpretation
- Based on the available evidence
- Within the scope of the corpus

Do not use when:
- The statement is simply true or false without an explicit issue of certainty or scope; use the corresponding factual, conceptual, or procedural pattern.
- The statement concerns obligation rather than certainty; use Deontic Status Consistency.

4.10 DEONTIC STATUS CONSISTENCY
Classification: Cross-Cutting

Intent:
Verify that the binding force of a claim matches the reference. The relevant modalities include mandatory, advisory, permitted, and prohibited.

Claim-identification guidance:
Use this type when the response states what must, should, may, may not, or must not be done. Apply it alongside the content-oriented pattern that checks what the action or rule actually is.

Claim-type label:
Deontic Status Consistency

Criterion template:
The response presents [claim C] as [modality M].

Permitted modalities:
- Mandatory
- Advisory
- Permitted
- Prohibited

Generic examples:
- The response presents setting the API key as mandatory before the program can call the API.
- The response presents saving audit prompts as optional.
- The response presents exposing the API key in source control as prohibited.
- The response presents a recommended delay between requests as advisory rather than mandatory.

Classification cues:
- Must
- Shall
- Required
- Should
- Recommended
- May
- Optional
- Permitted
- Must not
- Prohibited

Classification rule:
Deontic Status Consistency ordinarily supplements another claim type:
- A mandatory fact or rule may also be a Factual Assertion.
- A mandatory step may also be Procedural Step Presence.
- A conditional prohibition may also require Procedural Guard.

Do not use when:
- The statement expresses confidence or uncertainty; use Epistemic Status Consistency.
- The statement describes a procedure without indicating binding force; use the appropriate procedural pattern only.

4.11 ARTIFACT STRUCTURE
Classification: Cross-Cutting

Intent:
Verify that a created deliverable has the form, organization, sections, fields, file layout, or output structure demanded by the prompt, independently of the truth of individual claims.

Claim-identification guidance:
Use this type for requirements governing response format or artifact composition rather than substantive domain content.

Claim-type label:
Artifact Structure

Criterion template:
The response contains [structural element E] satisfying [property P].

Generic examples:
- The response contains a title, claim list, rationale, and claim-type summary.
- The response is organized as a decision framework.
- The program writes one output file per Prompt ID.
- The output filename matches the corresponding response filename.
- The result is formatted as Markdown or JSON according to the requested schema.

Classification cues:
- Contains a section
- Uses a table
- Follows a schema
- Includes a heading
- Produces a file
- Matches a filename
- Uses a required order or layout
- Meets length or formatting constraints

Do not use when:
- The statement concerns whether a substantive claim is true; use the appropriate knowledge-dimension pattern.
- The statement concerns whether a citation is supplied and valid; use Citation.
- The structure itself is a procedural action rather than an artifact requirement; consider Procedural Step Presence as well.

4.12 CITATION
Classification: Cross-Cutting

Intent:
Verify that citations or supporting references are provided consistently with the response.

Claim-identification guidance:
Use this type when the prompt, evaluator, or response requires a source, URL, footnote, bibliographic reference, quotation attribution, or other traceable support.

Claim-type label:
Citation

Criterion template:
The response cites [work or supporting material via text, URL, footnote, or other link].

Generic examples:
- The response cites the source paper.
- The response provides a museum collection URL supporting an artwork claim.
- The response attributes a quotation to its source.
- The response includes a bibliographic reference in the requested format.

Classification cues:
- Cites
- According to
- Source
- Reference
- URL
- Footnote
- Bibliography
- Attribution

Evaluation considerations:
- Presence of a citation.
- Correct association between citation and claim.
- Citation completeness where a format is specified.
- Citation support for the substantive claim.
- Absence of fabricated or irrelevant sources.

Do not use when:
- The statement merely names a work as part of substantive content without functioning as support; use Factual Assertion.
- The prompt does not require citation and the response makes no citation claim.

======================================================================
MULTI-LABEL CLASSIFICATION RULES
======================================================================

A single statement may instantiate more than one micro-pattern when it contains multiple independently testable dimensions.

Examples:

1. "The user must set OPENAI_API_KEY before running the script."
- Factual Assertion: The environment variable is named OPENAI_API_KEY.
- Procedural Ordering: The variable must be set before execution.
- Deontic Status Consistency: Setting it is mandatory.

2. "If an output file already exists, the program skips it unless --overwrite is supplied."
- Procedural Guard: File existence and the overwrite flag govern applicability.
- Procedural Step Presence: The program performs a skip action.
- Deontic Status Consistency: The default behavior and exception are represented with the correct binding force.

3. "A woodcut is a relief-printing process in which raised surfaces receive ink."
- Definitional: The statement defines woodcut.
- Conceptual Relation may also apply if the evaluator separately tests the relation between raised surfaces and ink transfer.

4. "The evidence suggests, but does not establish, that the technique was influenced by a specific tradition."
- Conceptual Relation: The claim relates the technique to an influence.
- Epistemic Status Consistency: The relation is presented as suggestive rather than established.

Classification procedure:
1. Divide the response into atomic claims.
2. Identify whether each claim is factual, conceptual, procedural, metacognitive, or cross-cutting.
3. Select the narrowest applicable criterion micro-pattern.
4. Add cross-cutting labels when the claim also asserts obligation, structure, or citation behavior.
5. Preserve the original claim's modality, scope, and certainty.
6. Do not classify a claim by whether it is true; classification is descriptive.
7. Do not invent claims absent from the response.

======================================================================
ATOMIC CLAIM DECOMPOSITION RULES
======================================================================

Treat a statement as atomic when it expresses one independently testable proposition.

Split a sentence when:
- It contains multiple facts that could independently be true or false.
- It contains more than one relationship.
- It combines a step with a separate ordering constraint.
- It combines a substantive assertion with a separate obligation.
- It combines a claim with a separate citation requirement.
- It contains a factual assertion and an epistemic qualification that must be tested independently.

Do not split when:
- Removing a condition would change the meaning of the claim.
- The components jointly form a single definition.
- The components jointly express one indivisible relation.
- A modifier is necessary to preserve scope, time, or applicability.

Exclude from the claim list unless substantively meaningful:
- Headings
- Greetings
- Transitional phrases
- Purely stylistic language
- Repetitions
- Questions that assert no proposition
- Empty disclaimers
- Formatting markers

RECOMMENDED OUTPUT FORMAT FOR CLAIM CLASSIFICATION
======================================================================

For each claim, return:

Claim [number]
- Claim: [atomic claim]
- Knowledge dimension: [Factual | Conceptual | Procedural | Metacognitive | Cross-Cutting]
- Criterion micro-pattern: [pattern name]
- Secondary pattern(s): [optional]
- Rationale: [brief explanation]
- Response evidence: [exact or minimally edited passage]
- Epistemic status: [definite | qualified | uncertain | inferred | not applicable]
- Deontic status: [mandatory | advisory | permitted | prohibited | not applicable]

Then provide a summary count by criterion micro-pattern.
